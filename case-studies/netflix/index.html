Netflix: what to produce next

What should Netflix produce next, and where should it grow? An end-to-end exploratory analysis that turns a raw catalogue of 8,807 titles into content and market recommendations for business leaders.

The brief

What the business case asked

Every new show or movie is a large investment. Using the Netflix catalogue as of September 2021, the case asks whether Netflix is shifting from movies to TV, how content volume has changed over 30 years, and which genres, audiences and durations dominate.

It also looks at what is available in each country, which markets are rising, who the key actors and directors are, and when is the best time to launch a title.

Key findings

What the data showed

25% → 34%

Netflix is pivoting to TV

TV's share of new additions rose from 2018 to 2021. Titles released in 2021: 315 TV shows vs 277 movies.

2,016

Growth has plateaued

Additions peaked at 2,016 titles in 2019, then fell to 1,879 in 2020.

6.5%

A family gap

46% of the library is adult-rated and only 6.5% is for little kids, yet kids and family genres grew 66% to 100% from 2018 to 2020.

59%

Three markets dominate

The US (3,690), India (1,046) and the UK (806) account for 59% of titles.

1 → 42

New frontiers

Nigeria grew from 1 to 42 titles a year and Egypt from 3 to 54 (2018 to 2020). Japan and Korea grew 70-80%.

67%

Most shows end early

Two in three TV shows have exactly one season.

98 min

The sweet-spot movie

The median movie runs 98 minutes, with the middle 50% between 87 and 114. Newer films are getting shorter.

35%

Friday is launch day

35% of TV shows and 26% of movies are added on a Friday. July and December are the peak months for TV.

Country personalities

Each market wants something different

This is why the recommendation is to go local, country by country, rather than run one global content plan.

Recommendations

What the business should do

1

Make more TV series

Especially international dramas, crime and kids' shows.

2

Renew good shows

Two in three stop after season 1, so set clear renewal rules for shows people finish.

3

Grow family and kids' content

Only 1 in 15 titles is for little kids, yet it is one of the fastest-growing areas.

4

Go local, country by country

Match each market's taste, and partner with studios in fast-growing Nigeria and Egypt.

5

Keep movies at 90-110 minutes

Kids' movies should run around an hour.

6

Launch big shows on a Friday in July or December

Avoid February for big launches.

7

Invest in growing genres

Reality, romance, action and kids. Be selective with stand-up and documentaries.

8

Co-produce to enter new markets

15% of titles already involve two or more countries.

9

Add well-known classic films selectively

They attract older audiences cheaply.

10

Pair this view with viewing data

Review these bets every quarter.

Method

How I did the analysis

Cleaning

Fixed 3 rows where duration was typed into rating, trimmed text, parsed date_added and split duration into a number and a unit.

Feature engineering

Added year, month and weekday added, years to Netflix, release era, and an audience group that maps 14 ratings to 4 groups.

Missing values

Director is 29.9% missing (91% of TV shows), cast and country 9.4% each. Filled with "Unknown" and excluded from rankings, with no rows dropped.

Un-nesting

Exploded multi-valued fields to one row per value, giving 36,439 actors, 4,993 directors, 122 countries and 42 genres.

Data types

Converted key columns to ordered categories, cutting memory from 9.7 MB to 7.5 MB.

Outliers

Used IQR fences on movie duration (7.3% outliers), seasons, release year and years to Netflix. Kept them as genuine titles and reported medians.

An honest limitation

The data shows what is available on Netflix, not what people watch. Without viewing hours or revenue, more titles signals Netflix's investment choices rather than proven demand. 2021 covers January to September only.
